Tinkoff · Senior Java Developer & de-facto delivery manager · currency-conversion platform · May 2022 – Jan 2023

Tinkoff: preventing FX rate gaming

~$7Mbuilt after the incident

Problem

The bank sets different FX rates and spreads for different client groups and currency pairs. If cross-rates or spreads don't line up, a client can chain conversions (e.g. A→B→C→A) and lock in a risk-free profit at the bank's expense. That is called rate gaming, or arbitrage. The risk was real: in early 2022, before I joined, cross-rate errors cost the bank an estimated ~$7M according to press reports. The courts later sided with the clients, so that money couldn't be clawed back. The fix had to stop it from happening in the first place.

What I did

  • Built an automated gaming check that continuously prices conversion cycles across currencies with the live client rates and flags any cycle that returns more than it started with.
  • When a cycle is exploitable, the service identifies the faulty rate, restores a correct one automatically and sends a critical alert to the team; every case is tracked as a metric.
  • Implemented flexible rate and spread settings by client group, giving the business finer control over FX margins.
  • Rolled out new currencies to client segments.
  • Stack: Java, Spring Boot, Kafka, PostgreSQL, Kubernetes, Jenkins · 6 microservices, 100+ operations/s
  • Was the team's de-facto delivery manager for several months: owned every production release of the currency-conversion team — planning, coordination, safe rollout and incident response.

Result

  • ~$7Mestimated loss from a cross-rate incident before I joined: the risk the control closes
  • 100+ FX operations/sprotected in real time
  • Auto-fixfaulty rate restored automatically, with a critical alert
  • Rates & spreads by client groupfiner control of FX margin

Raiffeisen Bank · Senior Java Developer · trading-risk platform · Jan 2023 – Apr 2025

Raiffeisen Bank: online control of trading deals

8 s → 500 mslimit-utilisation dashboard

Problem

The bank's trading desk closes B2B deals of several kinds: settlement, derivatives, money market. Two separate needs sat on the trading-risk platform. The sales desk needed to see each client's limit utilisation across all their deals; the dashboard that showed it was built without a speed requirement and took about 8 seconds to compute. Risk also wanted every deal's price checked against the market at the moment of the deal. That check had to live inside the trading flow, which meant it had to be fast from day one, or the desk would simply not adopt it.

Part 1 · Limit-utilisation dashboard for the sales desk

  • Utilisation per client across all their deals (settlement, derivative, money market), with the breakdown sales people use when they talk to a client.
  • Reworked the calculation: batch loading of positions and limits instead of per-deal queries. 8 s → 500 ms, so the dashboard answers while the client is still on the phone.

Part 2 · Price check inside the deal flow

  • Built the market-conformity service as a step in the deal flow: deals and order-book quotes stream in over Kafka; for each deal the service takes a volume-weighted quote from the order book at deal time (configurable benchmark: time offset, cutoff, window), computes the deviation of the deal price and checks it against thresholds per side. Results are stored and exposed as metrics.
  • Made the rules configurable without a release: a check is created through an API, and the set of deals it applies to is written as a filter expression (product = 'FX_SPOT' AND counterParty IN (…)) parsed with an ANTLR grammar.
  • Designed for the latency the desk would accept: daily PostgreSQL partitions for quotes and order books created and dropped by schedulers, caching of configuration and quotes, Micrometer metrics in Prometheus, Helm charts for Kubernetes.
  • Stack: Java 21, Spring Boot, Kafka, PostgreSQL (partitioning), ANTLR, Micrometer / Prometheus, Kubernetes / Helm, GitLab CI

Result

  • 8 s → 500 mslimit utilisation across all of a client's deals, on the sales dashboard
  • At deal timeprice checked against the order book inside the deal flow, with configurable thresholds
  • Config, not coderisk managers add checks as filter expressions, no release needed

Eliryn · Founder · AI coaching games, Telegram bot + web · Aug 2025 – Apr 2026

Eliryn: AI-guided coaching games

15% → 73%of started games finished

Problem

Eliryn turns coaching and art-therapy techniques into short metaphorical games: a user answers questions about images and stories, and an AI guide helps turn the answers into ideas and a plan. The first version needed free-text answers at every step. Users of metaphorical cards are used to picking a card, not writing, so most people who opened a game never typed a first answer, and few of those who did reached the end.

What I did

  • Kept each game a scripted flow and used the LLM as a router: after every step it classifies the answer (full, partial, "give me ideas", "don't know", off-topic). Only a full answer moves the game forward; the other categories get a tailored follow-up and the user stays on the step. The script stays predictable, and the AI decides when to move on.
  • Lowered the effort to answer: tap a card, pick a point on a scale, or press "Give ideas" / "Don't know". The buttons skip the classifier call entirely, and ideas come from a stronger model.
  • Grouped games into a six-step journey with a progress map and a final AI-written "resource map" report.
  • Built the whole product: shared sessions across Telegram and the web, guest play before sign-up, subscriptions with auto-renewal via YooKassa, per-plan game and token limits, RU/EN.
  • Stack: LLM router with structured outputs, prompt pipelines, Telegram Bot API + web app, YooKassa subscriptions, event analytics

Result

Feb–Apr 2026 vs earlier traffic, test accounts excluded

  • 15% → 73%of users who started a game finished one
  • 6% → 44%of visitors who opened a game typed or chose a first answer
  • 90%of users who finished journey step 2 also finished step 5

Lesson: the biggest jump came from making answering easier. The AI decides when to move on; the user should be able to answer with a tap.

Lesson: before an AI feature ships, I run it offline against a fixed test set and see how it does.
